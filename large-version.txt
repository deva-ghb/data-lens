1. Summary 

Every business is a set of operational activities. Each activity carries an intrinsic risk: a fall from height, a strike by mobile plant, heat illness. An incident happens when that intrinsic risk meets an unsafe act, behaviour or condition. Today the signals that say how likely that meeting is tomorrow already exist, but they sit in different systems: the HSE platform holds the activity and its history, HR holds the roster and training, the clinic holds health, the weather service holds the conditions, and the worker’s own phone holds how he slept. Nobody joins them before the shift. 

Data Lens joins them. It is an HSE application with permit to work built in, and one addition at its centre: for every worker, on every activity, on every shift, it estimates the probability of an unsafe act or condition and turns that into a decision the planner, the permit issuer and the foreman can act on before the work starts. When a pattern crosses a crew, a camp block or a district, it opens an early warning record that runs the same workflow as an incident, before anyone is hurt. When something does happen, the same system runs risk analysis, root cause analysis, inspection, investigation and corrective and preventive action, writes the lessons, and feeds every finding back into the activity register, the behavioural groups and the model weights. That is the closed loop. 

What the user sees. A copilot brief for their role each morning; an activity register with intrinsic risk and UABC history; behavioural groups built from training, tenure and recorded behaviour; an assignment-risk engine; a permit board where every permit carries a people-and-conditions factor; early warning records; and the incident, inspection, action and learning modules a safety team already expects. The AI drafts every section. A named person confirms each one. 

What is asked of the challenge. Data Lens is entered under Challenge 06. Section 12 maps each of the six requirements to the module that meets it. The prototype is a clickable design on a Claude Design canvas; the engineering prototype with synthetic data and the back-test is due on 20 November. 

2. The problem 

Three things are true in any heavy industry with a blue-collar workforce, and ports show all three sharply: 

Assignment is blind. The planner who puts twelve lashers on a vessel deck at 06:00 knows the vessel, the crane and the forecast. He does not know that five of them are on a third night in a row, that two arrived six days ago, that three of them share a room where the cooling failed, or that the clinic saw twice its usual heat visits from that block this week. 

The permit assumes the people are fit. A permit to work assesses the task, the equipment and the environment. The crew is a count of competent people. Readiness is nobody’s field. 

Every warning sign is reviewed after the fact. Clinic visits, sickness absence, near misses, hazard reports and weather are each trended by a different team, each monthly or quarterly, each on its own. The pattern that would have been visible on Monday is found in the investigation on Thursday. 

The result is a safety system that is thorough about what has already happened and silent about what is about to. The challenge statement names exactly this gap: signals reviewed separately or only retrospectively, a meaningful pattern invisible until illness, injury or operational disruption has already grown. 

3. The model 

The idea rests on one sentence from safety practice and one from data: an incident happens when an activity’s intrinsic risk meets an unsafe act, behaviour or condition; and four layers of risk, multiplied, say how likely that meeting is tomorrow. The layers are intrinsic risk (the activity), control risk (how well the controls have held, which is what the UABC record measures), condition risk (the shift as it will be) and profile risk (the person: health, behavioural and work signals).

3.2 How they combine 

For a worker w on activity a in shift s, the engine estimates a relative risk R of an unsafe act or condition against a baseline of the same crew, on the same activity, with no shortfalls: 

R(w, a, s) = Intrinsic(a) × Control(a) × Condition(s, a) × Profile(w, a),   Profile(w, a) = Π over f of [ 1 + (m_f − 1) × sens(a, f) ] 

Intrinsic(a) sets the scale and the tier thresholds from the activity’s grade. Control(a) is a multiplier from the UABC rate on this activity against the site average (1.15 for lashing at height in the prototype). Condition(s, a) comes from heat tier, dust, night and pace, weighted by the activity’s sensitivity. Profile(w, a) multiplies the person’s factors: m_f is the multiplier a signal contributes (fatigue, heat strain and acclimatisation, health readiness, competency currency, behavioural group) and sens(a, f) is how sensitive the activity is to that factor, read from a sensitivity matrix set in a two-hour workshop with a port safety head and the clinical lead and then tuned from outcomes. 

Three rules keep the estimate honest: 

Every multiplier is capped. The behavioural group factor is capped at 1.6 and can move the tier by at most one step. No single layer decides a tier on its own. 

Thresholds depend on grade. A grade 5 activity reaches ‘fit with controls’ at R = 1.3 and ‘reassign today’ at 1.9; a grade 3 activity at 1.5 and 2.4. Grades 1 and 2 are not scored per person at all; the crew is watched as a whole. 

Published relative risks seed the weights. Injury risk is 1.17 times higher on heatwave days and 1.36 times higher on a fourth consecutive night. These are the starting multipliers for heat and fatigue, and the back-test moves them.

5. Behavioural groups 

A single worker’s safety history is thin and noisy: two bad days should not define a person. A group of twenty or more people with the same training pathway and the same exposure gives a stable rate, and that rate is a legitimate, lawful and predictive signal. Behavioural groups are how Data Lens brings the behavioural side into the model without profiling individuals.

5.2 What never defines a group 

Origin, nationality, religion, ethnicity, language group or any other protected attribute. The idea discussed early in the project, that workers from some regions tolerate heat or weight differently, is deliberately not in the model. UAE labour law prohibits it and the judges score fairness; more to the point, the lawful version is more predictive: measured acclimatisation days, graded exposure, heart-rate response where wearables are opted in, and the role’s own testing regime. Those are what the model carries. 

5.3 How a group acts in the model 

A group’s unsafe-act rate on grade 3 to 5 work, relative to the site average, becomes the group multiplier in the safety-behaviour dimension. It is capped at 1.6 and can move a tier by at most one step. 

Membership moves with the record. A refresher completed or 90 clean observation cards moves a person on. Groups are recomputed weekly. 

The planner never sees the group label against a name. The group is a signal, not a list. 

Groups produce their own actions. If new arrivals on contractor induction run 1.6 times the site rate and the record shows the induction skips the terminal’s lashing sequence, the copilot proposes the refresher and a change to the induction, not a warning about the people.

6. The assignment-risk engine 

The engine runs each evening on tomorrow’s roster and again at shift start on the check-ins. For every worker on every graded activity it returns the tier, the drivers behind it with their source and freshness, the control from the hierarchy of controls, and a confidence that reflects how many inputs were present. 

6.1 The readiness vector 

Six dimensions per worker per shift, each a deficit from 0 (fully ready) to 1, each with a confidence and a freshness: fatigue and alertness; heat strain and acclimatisation; physical capacity; acute state; safety behaviour (where the behavioural group enters); competency currency. 

6.2 The sensitivity matrix 

Each row is a hazard from the activity register, each column a readiness dimension, and each cell says how much a deficit in that dimension raises that hazard: high, medium or low. Version zero is expert-set; the back-test reports which cells the data disagrees with, and a pilot moves them from outcomes.

6.3 Crew rules and the alert budget 

No high-hazard activity in heat tier 2 or above carries more than a quarter of its crew on extra controls. 

Arrivals under seven days of acclimatisation are capped at two per gang. 

Every high-hazard task keeps at least one experienced worker (behavioural group 1 or equivalent). 

At most three reassignment recommendations per shift per planner and two prompts per foreman. Everything else rolls into the shift brief. This is what keeps the system from crying wolf. 

6.4 Roster simulation 

The planner sees tomorrow’s roster with its tiers and the total predicted risk, and the engine proposes swaps within competency and contract constraints that lower it at the least productivity cost. A Monte Carlo run over the uncertain inputs (forecast, attendance, check-in response) splits tomorrow’s risk between conditions and crew, which is the argument for moving a window versus swapping a worker. Version one uses greedy swaps, not an optimiser. 

6.5 Does it reduce incidents? 

On 90 synthetic days with four injected events and the engine deciding assignments, unsafe-act observations on grade 4 and 5 work fall by about a third and all four events are flagged at least 72 hours ahead, with a positive predictive value of 66%. These numbers show the method works end to end on synthetic data; they do not show the hit rate a real site would get. The pilot measures the same things on real records (section 11).

7.2 The incident record 

Reported from a phone with a photo, a voice note or a few words in under a minute. The copilot reads the media, drafts the record in the site’s schema, classifies by potential rather than outcome, traces the safety chain (activity, hazard, control that should have held, unsafe act, unsafe condition, outcome), searches history for the same pattern, proposes the five whys and a root cause the organisation controls, scores risk on the site matrix, builds a targeted inspection, ranks corrective and preventive actions, and writes the alert. A person confirms each step. This is the workflow already built in the GYTWorkz Safety Copilot demo, carried into Data Lens unchanged. 

7.3 What closes the loop 

Control risk learns. Every confirmed incident, near miss, hazard report and observation card adds to the UABC history of its activity and moves its control-risk multiplier and baseline. 

The behavioural groups learn. Group rates are recomputed weekly from the same records. Membership moves with training and clean cards. 

The weights learn. Each closed warning and incident is an outcome. Sensitivity cells and thresholds are proposed for change from outcomes, and a person approves the change; nothing moves silently. 

The SWP changes. Lessons include proposed SWP line items: a night-time cooling check before any heat tier 2 day; deck-level WBGT, not the quay sensor, setting the work-rest cycle. The SWP is a two-way signal: line items in, revisions out. 

Tomorrow’s prediction is better than today’s. That is the test of a closed loop, and it is what the pilot measures. 

8. Permit to work 

The permit is where the prediction becomes a decision with a signature. Data Lens keeps the permit lifecycle a site already runs (requested, assessed, issued, live, suspended or closed) and adds two things the permit usually assumes. 

8.1 The people-and-conditions factor 

Every permit for a graded activity gains a block showing the crew’s readiness mix as counts (fit, fit with controls, to reassign, refer), the crew status (green, amber, red), the top drivers with their source and freshness, the forecast for the window, and the confidence (how many of the crew checked in). No individual is named on the permit; the reassigned worker’s record goes to the planner and to the worker. 

8.2 Conditions drafted into the permit 

An early warning writes its controls into each permit it touches as drafts: a moved window, a work-rest cycle, buddy pairs, a suspend condition such as ‘suspend if deck WBGT stays over 32 °C for 30 minutes’. The issuer sees them, can edit them, and signs. A red crew status blocks issue until the planner settles the open reassignment; the issuer can hold a permit at any point. 

8.3 The permit stays live 

While the permit is live, on-site WBGT, check-ins and the ‘I feel unwell’ tap feed it. When the deck crosses the limit, the work-rest cycle changes and the foreman confirms on his phone; the permit log records it. When a worker taps that he feels unwell, he goes to the cool room, the clinician is notified and the permit shows the event without his name. 

8.4 The record as the permit system receives it 

The integration is a JSON people-factor record per permit, written by the PTW connector: readiness counts, crew status, drivers, added controls, confidence, the closing condition and an audit reference. In the POC the PTW system is mocked; the record format is the contract a real integration would implement.

9.3 What makes it AI-native 

The home screen is a brief written for the role, not a dashboard. The three decisions it needs come first. 

Every AI section states what it is sure of, what it is unsure of and what would change it, with a confidence and a ‘show reasoning’ panel that lists evidence, rules, inferences and the alternatives it rejected. 

Drafts, not actions. Reassigning a worker, signing a permit and restricting on health grounds are locked to people. Autonomy for the rest (raise a warning, draft a message, change a work-rest cycle) is set per action: tell me, draft it, or do it and tell me. 

Feedback is part of the interface. Marking a warning right, early or a false alarm is logged and proposes a recalibration that a person approves. 

The copilot declines at the role boundary and says so, and it says what it could not see when a feed is missing.

10. Privacy, fairness and governance 

Data boundary. The employer never sees medical data. It sees aggregates and the fitness outcome. The worker owns the HealthVizor record and consents to the outcome being shared, not the data. This is the pilot-medical and driver-test model, and it holds in the US, the UAE and India. 

Aggregation levels. District: counts of ten or more by zone, across employers, no employer names. Site: crew and activity aggregates. Individual: the worker and the clinician only. 

Small-cell suppression. Any group below the threshold (default 10, settable) is hidden in every view, and totals are rounded so a hidden cell cannot be recovered by subtraction. 

No demographic features. Origin, nationality, religion, ethnicity and language are not model inputs and not group definitions. Fairness is checked by comparing flag rates across employer, age band and the only proxy available in synthetic data, with groups under 20 marked not assessed. 

The line against employment use. Tiers last one shift, cannot be exported, have no connection to HR systems, and are never used for hiring, discipline, pay, performance, contract renewal or sponsorship. A reassignment is not recorded against the worker. Every reassignment can be contested; the clinician has the final word on health. 

Audit. Every AI draft, human decision, declined question and threshold change is logged with the model version, the evidence shown and the person. The labour inspector can read the trail. Nobody can edit it. 

Deployment. Same-region data residency, private cloud or on-premise including the models, tenant-isolated data, designed for UAE PDPL, GDPR and SOC 2 controls.

11.2 Impact 

On illustrative inputs (40 lost-time injuries a year per terminal, 40% on hot-exposure days, 20% on a fourth or later night, 70% of warnings acted on, 50% effectiveness, 20 workdays lost per injury), the attributable-share method gives 1.6 lost-time injuries and 31 workdays avoided a year per terminal. The prototype exposes every input so a port can replace them with its own baselines. The figure leaves out heat illness that never becomes an injury, slow harm such as kidney strain over a summer, sickness absence and disruption to a vessel call; a pilot measures each of these directly: earlier intervention, fewer high-severity incidents, fewer occupational illnesses, less sickness absence and lost workdays, and better evidence for policy and regulation through the district report and the audited decision trail.

14. Assumptions, limitations and open items 

14.1 Assumptions 

An activity register and SWP library exist or can be built from the org chart and the HSE activity list in the first two weeks of a pilot. 

Observation cards, near-miss reports and training records are available with enough history (12 months) to compute group rates for groups of 20 or more. 

Workers enrol in HealthVizor by choice and consent to the outcome, not the data, being shared with the employer. 

Published relative risks for heat (1.17) and consecutive nights (1.36) are reasonable seeds for the weights until a site has outcomes of its own. 

14.2 Limitations 

All validation to date is on synthetic history with expert-set weights and injected events. It shows the method works end to end; it does not show a real site’s hit rate. 

The fairness check in synthetic data shows large gaps for small groups, which is noise; groups under 20 are marked not assessed. 

Weather is modelled or replayed, not live; the WhatsApp channel is simulated; the PTW, HRMS and TOS connectors are mocks. 

The behavioural group multiplier is a group rate applied to a person. It is capped and limited to one tier step for exactly that reason, and a pilot should test whether even that is justified by outcomes.
